How it works

    

      
What aggregate planning is

      
Aggregate planning sets production for the next several months in broad strokes: not which product runs on which line, but how many people to employ, how much to make each month, and where to put the gap between what the plant can make and what customers want. Demand rarely sits still, and every way of dealing with that costs something. Hiring and layoffs cost money, overtime pays a premium, stock built early has to be stored, and orders filled late cost goodwill.

      
Level, chase, and the band between

      
A level plan keeps the same workforce all year, builds inventory in the slow months and draws it down in the busy ones. A chase plan changes the workforce to follow demand, so inventory stays low but hiring and layoffs pile up. A band lets the workforce move only within limits you set, which is how most plants actually work. The cheapest plan usually mixes all of these: a bit of overtime here, some early stock there, one hire before the peak.

      
An exact optimizer, not a spreadsheet heuristic

      
The planner writes the problem as a mixed-integer program: every month has a workforce balance, an inventory balance, an overtime limit and a capacity limit, and the objective adds up hiring, layoffs, regular time, overtime, holding, backlog and material. It hands that to HiGHS, an open-source solver compiled to WebAssembly, which searches until it can prove no cheaper plan exists. With “Whole teams only” off, the same model is a linear program and solves almost instantly.

      
Everything runs on this page, and the numbers you enter stay in your browser. The share link carries them in the part of the address after the “#”, which browsers never send to a server. The source is on GitHub: model.js holds the whole model.
