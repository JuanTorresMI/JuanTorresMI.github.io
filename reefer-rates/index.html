How it works

{% if r %}Copied {{ r.generated | date: "%b %-d, %Y" }}{% endif %}

Every week the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s market news reporters ask shippers, receivers and truck brokers what it’s costing to move produce, and publish the answer as the Specialty Crops National Truck Rate Report. It’s the most consistent public record of reefer spot rates there is, but it comes as a PDF or as raw rows through an API, one row per commodity.

  1. A Cloudflare Worker reads the report. The same small Worker behind this site’s YouTube page asks USDA’s MyMarketNews API for the newest report every six hours, and stores it in a SQLite database on Cloudflare (D1) once it’s new. The key it uses is free, read-only, and kept as an encrypted secret, never in this site’s code.
  2. It boils the rows down to lanes. USDA repeats each rate once per commodity it covers, so the same number shows up dozens of times. The Worker keeps each distinct rate a shipping area quotes to a city once. Usually there’s one; sometimes an area quotes a second rate for a group of crops, citrus most often, and that shows on the board as “also quoted”.
  3. It fills in the past by itself. In the hours between new reports it asks for one older week at a time, walking back until USDA has nothing older. That’s where the history on each lane page comes from{% if r %}: right now it goes back to the week of {{ r.first }}{% endif %}.
  4. Once a day, this page is rebuilt from it. A GitHub Action copies the Worker’s data into the site and rebuilds it, including a page for every lane and the CSV download. So everything here is plain HTML: it loads instantly and reads the same with or without JavaScript.

How to read the board: the range is the lowest to highest rate reported for a full load in a 48 to 53 foot refrigerated trailer, broker fees included. Mostly is where most loads actually moved, when USDA reports it. Change is USDA’s own figure for the move from the week before. The truck label is USDA’s read on supply in that area that week, from surplus (more trucks than loads) to shortage (more loads than trucks).

Shipping areas come and go with the growing seasons, so a lane missing from the board this week is usually just out of season. Its page stays up with its history, and lists the months it has been quoted in.

Diesel is the U.S. average retail price of No. 2 diesel from the Energy Information Administration, also weekly. It’s here for context: most fuel surcharge tables are built on it.

Source: USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, Specialty Crops Market News. The numbers are theirs and are public; any mistakes in presenting them are mine. The Worker’s code is in the worker folder of this site’s repository.
